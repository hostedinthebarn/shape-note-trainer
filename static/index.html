What it is

Sing the Shapes is a practice app for singers who read shape notes. It plays you do, shows you a short exercise, and listens through your phone's microphone while you sing it.

It waits on every note. Each one is judged against the do you were given, so if you drift you can hear that you drifted. There is no timer and no pass mark to chase. You sing until the note is right, or you skip it and move on.

There are 8 graded levels, in treble and bass clef, so sopranos, altos, tenors and basses each read from their own line.

Why we built it

Congregations that sing from Songs of Faith and Praise read 7-shape (Aikin) notation. Each note head has a shape, and the shape tells you which note of the scale it is. Once you know the seven shapes, you can find your note without a piano.

Learning them takes practice, and we couldn't find anything to practise with. FaSoLaMix is a good app, but it is built for 4-shape Sacred Harp and teaches by ear. The sight-singing apps that listen to you use round notes. Nothing drilled 7-shape reading with live feedback on your pitch, so we made it.

It matters to Gary, who runs Hosted in the Barn, for a personal reason. He didn't grow up in the Church of Christ, but he loves to sing. Learning the shapes lets him sight-read our hymnals and sing the tenor part, rather than only following the melody.

It started as a set of printable drills with answer keys, and grew into this app.

How it works

  1. Pick your part. Soprano, alto, tenor or bass. That sets your clef and a comfortable do. In Songs of Faith and Praise the tenor reads the bass clef, and so does the app.
  2. Listen to do, then sing. The app plays do. You sing the notes of the exercise one at a time, and the app marks each note as you go.
  3. See how you did. Every note is marked on its own: unaided, helped, or skipped. At the end you get a short summary and the notes that gave you trouble.

A note counts when you hold it in tune for about a third of a second. "In tune" means within half a semitone of the right pitch. A strict setting narrows that to a quarter of a semitone, if you want it. If you're stuck, you can replay do at any time, and after 8 seconds the app offers to play the note itself.

The levels

A level is a set of four-bar exercises, each in a different key. Do moves around the staff, and the shapes stay the same.

  1. Do, re, mi by step
  2. Do up to sol by step
  3. The full scale by step
  4. Do, mi, sol, do: the chord leaps
  5. Skips of a third
  6. Below do: low sol, la and ti, with half notes
  7. Fourths and fifths
  8. Hymn-style phrases with dotted rhythms

Levels are never locked. After 3 clean attempts in a row, the app suggests moving up, and you can ignore it.

Before you start

  • Allow the microphone. Your browser asks once. The app can't hear you without it. On an iPhone, an app added to the Home Screen asks again each time it opens.
  • Find a quiet room. The app listens for one clear voice, so background noise and other singers nearby make it harder to read your pitch.
  • Add it to your Home Screen if you want it to open like an app. After the first visit it works with no signal at all.

We've tested it on an iPhone. We haven't been able to try it on Android yet, so tell us how it goes.

Your voice stays on your phone

The pitch is worked out on your phone, in the browser. The app has no code that records or uploads audio. Your settings and practice history are kept in your browser's storage on that phone, and nowhere else. We count visits to the site with Cloudflare's cookie-free analytics, which sees a page being opened and nothing you sing or practise.

How it was built

Claude planned the app and wrote the tests. A local model running on a laptop then wrote the core of it, one module at a time, until every test passed. We wrote up how that went on the Hosted in the Barn blog. The code is on GitHub under the MIT licence.
